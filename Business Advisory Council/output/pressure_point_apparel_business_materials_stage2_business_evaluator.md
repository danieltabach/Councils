## Agreements

**Advisor E (Strategy) — Sale pricing undermines brand positioning**

Advisor E flags the perpetual "Sale" badges as a strategic coherence problem. I agree, and I want to add the financial dimension that E doesn't fully develop: this isn't just a brand signal problem, it's a margin architecture problem. The tees were originally priced at $30. At $30, the true net on a Logo Tee is approximately $18.57 — a workable margin. At $20 (the current "sale" price), it drops to $8.57. That's a 54% reduction in net profit per unit by running a permanent discount. If the $20 price is the real price, remove the strikethrough and price it correctly. If $30 is the real price, restore it and stop training customers to expect a discount. The financial cost of this ambiguity is measurable and ongoing.

**Advisor G (Conversion) — Shipping cost visibility is a checkout abandonment trigger**

G correctly identifies that undisclosed shipping costs at checkout are the #1 cause of cart abandonment across ecommerce. I flagged the shipping subsidy problem from a margin perspective in my original review; G's point that the *customer-facing* presentation of shipping costs is also broken is an important complement. These are two separate problems with the same root cause: the shipping economics were never properly modeled before launch. The fix G recommends — displaying estimated shipping on the cart page before checkout — is correct and costs nothing to implement.

**Advisor G (Conversion) — Empty reviews section is worse than no reviews section**

This is a finding I should have flagged more explicitly in my original review. From a financial modeling standpoint, conversion rate is a direct input into revenue projections. An empty reviews section suppresses conversion rate, which means every unit of inventory takes longer to sell, which means capital is tied up longer, which extends the cash flow gap. The 33 people who received gifted or sample product are an untapped review asset. Reaching out to all of them this week costs nothing and could materially improve conversion rate within days.

**Advisor C (Customer) — "This brand needs to be felt before it's bought"**

This customer insight is the most financially important observation in the entire advisory council, and it directly validates the gym partnership strategy from a unit economics perspective. If the gym channel can deliver a physical touchpoint that converts at meaningfully higher rates than cold DTC traffic, then the effective CAC through gym partnerships is dramatically lower than through any digital channel. A customer who touches the product at a gym and then buys online has near-zero acquisition cost. This is the financial argument for prioritizing gym partnerships above all other channels.

**Advisor H (Marketing) — The AFSP commitment is an underutilized marketing asset**

H makes this point from a marketing perspective. I want to make it from a financial one: the AFSP commitment is a pricing justification tool that is not being used. A brand with a genuine, formalized charity commitment can command a small price premium over an equivalent brand without one. Research on cause-related marketing consistently shows 5-15% willingness-to-pay increases when the charitable commitment is prominent and credible. At current price points, even a $2-3 price premium on tees (from $20 to $22-23) would improve net margin by 23-35% per unit. The commitment is already made. The financial benefit of surfacing it prominently is being left on the table.

**Advisor F (Market) — The women's segment is being under-resourced relative to its inventory commitment**

F recommends focusing 90% of marketing energy on the primary male segment. I agree on financial grounds: 11 Women's Logo Tees with zero units sold represents dead capital at $75.35 COGS. There is no marketing strategy visible for this segment, which means the inventory investment has no corresponding demand-generation investment. Either allocate marketing resources to move this inventory or accept that it will remain unsold and factor that into future drop planning decisions. Carrying inventory that has no marketing support is a capital efficiency problem.

## Disagreements

**Advisor H (Marketing) — TikTok as the "primary organic acquisition channel" framing**

H recommends TikTok as the highest-priority channel and frames it as the solution to the organic discovery problem. I don't disagree with the channel recommendation itself — TikTok's discovery algorithm is genuinely superior to Instagram's for new brands. My disagreement is with the implicit financial assumption: that organic TikTok reach will translate to meaningful revenue at this stage.

Here is the financial reality: even if a TikTok video reaches 50,000 people, the conversion path from TikTok view to Shopify purchase involves multiple steps, a brand with zero social proof, and products that — as Advisor C correctly identifies — need to be felt before they're bought. The organic-to-revenue conversion rate for a new DTC apparel brand on TikTok without reviews, without UGC, and without social proof is likely in the 0.1-0.3% range on video views. At 50,000 views, that's 50-150 site visitors, converting at perhaps 1-3% to purchase, yielding 1-4 sales per viral video.

TikTok should absolutely be launched. But it should be understood as a brand awareness and email list building tool at this stage, not a revenue driver. The gym partnership channel, by contrast, converts at dramatically higher rates because the trust infrastructure already exists in the gym community. The financial priority stack should be: gym partnerships first (highest conversion rate, lowest CAC), TikTok second (brand awareness, list building), paid ads third (when margins and creative are ready).

**Advisor E (Strategy) — "The 'limited drops' identity claim isn't yet operationally real"**

E flags the gap between the scarcity narrative and the 363 units of unsold inventory. I partially agree with the observation but disagree with the implication that the drop model itself is the problem. The drop model is financially correct for a brand at this stage — it limits inventory risk, creates purchase urgency, and allows for small-batch production testing. The problem is not the drop model; it's the sell-through rate within each drop.

The financial fix is not to abandon the drop model but to right-size drop quantities to match realistic demand. The dashboard decision rule Daniel built is exactly right: "Do not approve a drop if break-even units exceed expected sell-through units." The issue is that this rule wasn't applied rigorously to earlier drops — 100 Stay Ready Oversized Tees with zero sales is the clearest example. Future drops should be sized at 20-30 units until sell-through velocity is established, then scaled up. The scarcity narrative becomes true when drop quantities are calibrated to actual demand.

**Advisor B (Creative) — The thank you card at $0.07 is "underutilized"**

B recommends improving the thank you card copy as a high-ROI brand investment. I don't disagree with the creative recommendation, but I want to add a financial nuance: at $0.07 per card, the packaging cost is already extremely lean. The ROI opportunity B is describing is not about spending more on packaging — it's about getting more value from what's already being spent. The card already exists. Rewriting the copy costs nothing. This is a zero-cost improvement that should happen immediately, but it shouldn't be confused with a packaging investment decision.

Where I would push back on B more directly: the suggestion to improve packaging quality to match the brand's "small-batch quality" positioning is premature from a cash flow standpoint. At 39 units sold and -$1,913 net position, increasing per-unit packaging costs — even by $0.50-$1.00 — is not the right financial priority. The packaging investment conversation becomes relevant when the brand is doing 100+ orders per month and has established positive cash flow. Right now, the $0.42 per unit is the correct spend.
