## AGREEMENTS

### Advisor A — Strategy Consultant

**Agreement: The "Sale" badges are actively undermining the brand positioning.**
Advisor A flags this as the most urgent fix, and I agree completely. I raised it in my original review under Channel Strategy and Content Strategy, but I understated its severity. A brand that explicitly positions itself as "quiet, composed, and mid-premium" cannot run perpetual sale pricing across 100% of its catalog. From a marketing standpoint, this is not just a pricing problem — it is a *messaging problem*. Every "Sale" badge is a signal that contradicts the brand's stated identity. The customer who resonates with "Pressure Is a Privilege" is being told, simultaneously, "but we couldn't sell it at full price." Those two signals cannot coexist without eroding trust. Advisor A is right that this needs to be resolved before any other conversion work matters.

**Agreement: The AFSP commitment needs dramatically more surface area.**
Advisor A identifies this as Gap 3 in their Mission-to-Execution section. I called it "the single most underutilized marketing asset in your entire business" in my original review. We are in full alignment here, and the convergence of two different advisors on the same point should signal to Rosa and Daniel that this is not a minor tweak — it is a structural brand asset that is currently buried. The fact that it appears in the footer and the Our Story page, but not in the hero section, not on product pages, and not in the email sequence, means the brand's most emotionally resonant differentiator is invisible at every high-intent moment in the customer journey.

**Agreement: The "limited drops" identity claim is not yet operationally real.**
Advisor A raises this in Gap 2. I did not address this directly in my original review, and I should have. From a marketing perspective, claiming scarcity while sitting on 363 units of unsold inventory is a credibility risk. The drop model is the right long-term architecture. But the marketing language around it — "limited," "small batch," "exclusive" — needs to be earned through actual sell-through velocity before it becomes a central brand claim. Until then, the brand should lead with identity and community, not scarcity.

**Agreement: The gym-first distribution model is strategically correct.**
Advisor A validates this as the highest-conviction near-term channel. I agree, and I want to add the marketing dimension: the gym is not just a sales channel — it is a *trust transfer mechanism*. When a coach at Riverfront Jiu-Jitsu wears a Pressure Point rashguard, every student who sees it is receiving a credibility endorsement that no Instagram post can replicate. The mat is the right first touchpoint. The website is the right second touchpoint. The current strategy has these in the wrong order for most potential customers.

### Advisor B — Customer Perspective

**Agreement: Zero reviews is the single biggest trust gap for a first-time buyer.**
Advisor B's review is the most practically useful document in this council for understanding why conversion is failing. The observation that "I would not enter my credit card today, but I'm close" is exactly the signal I was trying to surface in my Messaging Gaps section. The brand is close. The gap is trust infrastructure, not brand identity. Reviews are the fastest, cheapest way to close that gap. Advisor B's specific framing — "I need one real review from a real person saying something specific" — is more actionable than anything I wrote on this topic.

**Agreement: The "pressure-reveal fabric" claim on the rashguard needs visual proof.**
Advisor B flags this as a confusion point: "Is this a real feature of the fabric? What does it look like? I need a photo or video of this actually happening or I'm going to assume it's marketing language." This is a significant marketing gap I missed entirely. If the pressure-reveal feature is real and demonstrable, it is the single most differentiated product claim in the entire catalog. A 15-second TikTok video showing this feature in action — someone rolling, the pressure points appearing, fading as they recover — would be the most compelling product content the brand could create. The fact that this feature exists but is not being demonstrated is a major missed opportunity.

**Agreement: The "Drop" structure is confusing to new visitors.**
Advisor B asks: "Does Drop 001 sell out and disappear? Are Drop 001 products still available? Can I buy from multiple drops at once?" I did not address this in my original review. From a marketing standpoint, the drop model requires explanation for customers who are not already familiar with streetwear drop culture. A single sentence of context — "Each drop is a limited collection. Available while supplies last." — would resolve this confusion without requiring any structural changes.

**Agreement: The gym partnership strategy makes sense from the customer's perspective.**
Advisor B's closing insight — "this brand needs to be felt before it's bought" — is the most important customer-side validation of the gym partnership strategy I've seen in this council. It is not just a distribution insight. It is a marketing insight. The brand's tactile, physical qualities (the rashguard fabric, the fit, the weight of the cotton) are conversion arguments that photography cannot make. The gym is the only channel where those arguments can be made.

### Advisor C — Financial/Operational

**Agreement: The Apply the Pressure Tee has dangerously thin margins at current pricing.**
Advisor C's fully-loaded cost analysis shows true net of $3.82 on the Apply the Pressure Tee at $20 retail. I referenced thin tee margins in my original review but did not quantify them this precisely. From a marketing standpoint, this has direct implications: this product should not be the one you're discounting, gifting in bulk, or using as a loss-leader to drive gym partnerships. The rashguard and fight shorts — with $17+ true net — are the products that can support marketing investment. The tee is a brand-building product, not a margin-building product.

**Agreement: The welcome sequence is the highest-ROI fix available.**
Advisor C and I are in complete alignment on this. I outlined a three-email welcome sequence in my original review. Advisor C validates the financial logic: every subscriber who doesn't receive a welcome sequence is a warm lead that goes cold. At current traffic volumes, every email address is precious. The cost to build this sequence is one afternoon of Rosa's time. The return compounds indefinitely.

### Advisor D — Market Analyst

**Agreement: Concentrate on the primary male grappler segment for the next 6-12 months.**
Advisor D makes the segmentation argument clearly: the Women's Logo Tee has sold zero units despite being in inventory. The market is telling you something. I agree with this recommendation from a marketing resource allocation standpoint. A 2-person team with no ad budget cannot run two parallel content strategies for two meaningfully different customer segments. Every piece of content, every gym partnership, every influencer gift should be optimized for the primary customer first. The women's line should remain in the product catalog but should not receive dedicated marketing energy until the primary segment achieves consistent monthly revenue.

**Agreement: The indirect competitor set is broader than the brand acknowledges.**
Advisor D flags Amazon rashguards and generic athletic streetwear as the real competitive threat at the awareness stage. This is a marketing insight I should have addressed more directly. The customer who doesn't know Pressure Point exists isn't choosing between Pressure Point and Tatami — they're choosing between Pressure Point and the Amazon rashguard they found in 30 seconds. The brand's marketing needs to address this default behavior, not just the branded competitor set.
