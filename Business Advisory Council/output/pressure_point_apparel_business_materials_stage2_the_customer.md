## Agreements

**Advisor A (Conversion Architect) — The "Sale" badge problem.**
Yes. A hundred times yes. I flagged this in my review but Advisor A articulated *why* it's a conversion problem more precisely than I did: perpetual sale pricing trains customers to see the sale price as the real price, which makes the original price feel fake. From where I'm sitting as a customer, I'll add something simpler — when everything is on sale, nothing feels special. If the rashguard is always $50, just price it at $50. The strikethrough $55 doesn't make me feel like I'm getting a deal. It makes me feel like I'm being played.

**Advisor A — No email popup or lead capture on the homepage.**
Completely agree. I scrolled right past the footer email field. I didn't even notice it until someone pointed it out. If you want my email, you have to ask for it at a moment when I'm actually paying attention — not buried at the bottom of the page after I've already decided whether I'm interested.

**Advisor A — The empty reviews section is worse than no reviews section.**
This is the one I wish I'd said more clearly. I mentioned that zero reviews is a problem, but Advisor A nailed the specific reason: an *empty* reviews section actively signals that nobody has bought this. It's not neutral. It's negative. If you can't fill it yet, hide it until you can.

**Advisor B (Creative Director) — The photography inconsistency.**
I said this too, but Advisor B gave me better language for it. The two visual languages — dark gritty underground brand vs. clean Shopify template — are fighting each other. As a customer scrolling through, I feel that friction even if I can't name it. It creates a vague sense that the brand isn't fully formed, which creates doubt right when I'm deciding whether to trust them with my money.

**Advisor B — The thank you card is underutilized.**
I didn't think about this at all in my original review, and I should have. The moment I open a package is actually the moment I'm most emotionally open to a brand. If the card inside says something that actually lands — something that sounds like a real person wrote it, not a template — that's the moment I'd probably post about it or tell someone. A $0.07 card that makes me feel something is better marketing than most of what's on the website.

**Advisor F (Strategy Consultant) — The "limited drops" claim vs. 363 unsold units.**
I didn't have visibility into the inventory numbers when I wrote my review, but this resonates with me as a customer. If a brand tells me something is "limited" and then it's still available three months later, I stop believing the scarcity signal. The next time they say "limited," I won't rush. This is a trust problem that compounds over time.

**Advisor H (Marketing) — The AFSP commitment is buried.**
This is the one that surprised me most when I read it, because I had the same reaction in my review — I said the mental health angle would make me more likely to buy — but I didn't realize how buried it actually is until Advisor H pointed it out. I found it on the Our Story page. Most customers won't read the Our Story page. This should be on the product page. It should be in the Instagram bio. It should be in the first email I get. It's the most emotionally resonant thing about this brand for the target customer, and it's being hidden.

**Advisor E (Market Analyst) — Focus on the primary male segment first.**
The Women's Logo Tee has sold zero units. As a customer, I can tell you I didn't even notice there was a women's product on the site. It's not being marketed, it's not being featured, and it's not being talked about. You can't serve two audiences with one person's bandwidth. Pick the one that's actually buying.

## Disagreements

**Advisor B — "The Drop 002 section with the heavy red background reads more Venum than Pressure Point."**
I actually don't agree with this one. When I first saw the homepage, the red section is what made me stop scrolling. It has energy. The rest of the page is very dark and quiet — which is correct for the brand — but without that red section, the homepage would feel flat. I think there's a difference between "loud" and "high contrast." Venum is loud because of the imagery and the messaging. The red section on this homepage is high contrast but the content inside it is still restrained. I'd keep it.

**Advisor A — "A timed popup (30 seconds, or on exit intent) offering something specific."**
I understand the logic here and I know it works statistically. But as a customer, popups make me feel like I'm being chased. The brand's whole identity is about composure and not being pushy. A popup that interrupts my browsing feels off-brand in a way that's hard to explain but real. If they're going to do lead capture, I'd rather see it done in a way that feels like an invitation — maybe a sticky bar at the top or bottom that stays visible without interrupting. Something that says "get early access to Drop 003" without jumping in my face. The mechanism matters for a brand that's built on restraint.

**Advisor F — The "sale" pricing fix should be to "restore the original price and run a genuine limited-time promotion."**
I agree the sale badges need to go. But I'm not sure the solution is to restore the $30 price on tees and then run a promotion. From where I'm standing, the $20-25 price point for tees is actually correct for this brand at this stage. The problem isn't the price — it's the presentation. Just price the tees at $25, remove the strikethrough, and let the price speak for itself. Don't manufacture urgency with a "limited time" promotion on products that have been sitting in inventory for months. That feels just as inauthentic as the perpetual sale.

## Revisions to My Review

**What I missed: The welcome sequence.**
Advisor H and Advisor A both flagged this, and they're right that I didn't address it. I was thinking about the experience of someone who visits the site and either buys or doesn't. I didn't think about the person who visits, gets interested, signs up for the email list, and then hears nothing. That person is actually closer to buying than a cold visitor — they've already raised their hand. And right now the brand goes silent on them. If I'd signed up for the email list and received a three-email sequence that told Rosa's story, explained the snake symbolism, and mentioned the AFSP commitment, I'd probably have bought within a week. That's a real miss in my original review.

**What I missed: The shipping cost transparency.**
I mentioned shipping costs as a concern but I didn't dig into the specific problem. Advisor A and Advisor G both pointed out that Rosa is absorbing $3.50-$5.00 per order, which means the customer is paying for shipping but not the full amount. The customer experience problem is that I don't know what shipping will cost until I'm at checkout. That's the moment I'm most likely to abandon. I should have been more specific: show me the shipping cost before I get to checkout, or give me a free shipping threshold I can work toward. The "buy a rashguard and a tee to get free shipping" math actually makes sense to me as a customer — I'd probably do it.

**What I'd strengthen: The "feel it before you buy it" insight.**
I said at the end of my review that "this brand needs to be felt before it's bought." I stand by that completely, and I think it's actually the most important thing I said. But I'd add something now: the gym partnership strategy isn't just about getting the product in front of people. It's about creating a moment where someone can touch the fabric, see the logo in person, and watch a coach they respect wearing it. That's worth more than any amount of Instagram content. The advisors who analyzed the gym strategy from a financial and strategic angle are right that it's the correct primary channel — but the reason it's correct is exactly what I said: this brand converts through physical experience, not digital browsing.

**What I'd add: The "fabric reveals pressure points" claim needs proof.**
I flagged this as a confusion point, but I want to be stronger about it. This is either the most interesting product feature I've ever seen on a rashguard, or it's marketing language that means nothing. I genuinely cannot tell which. If it's real — if the fabric actually shows contact points during a roll and fades as you recover — that's remarkable. That's the kind of thing I'd show my training partners. That's a TikTok video waiting to happen. But right now it's one sentence in a product description
