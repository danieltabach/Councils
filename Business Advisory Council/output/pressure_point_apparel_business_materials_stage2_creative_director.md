## Disagreements

**Advisor F (Conversion Architect) — "The product pages are the strongest part of the site"**

Advisor F gives the product pages significant credit and calls them "the strongest part of the site." I partially disagree. The *rashguard* product page is the strongest page on the site — it has multi-angle photography, on-model shots, compelling copy, and the most complete visual treatment. But the tee product pages are not strong. They have ghost mannequin photography on a smoky gray background that reads as commodity-tier. Calling the product pages collectively "the strongest part" obscures a critical disparity: the brand's highest-margin, highest-conversion-potential products (rashguards, fight shorts) are well-photographed; the most accessible entry-point products (tees) are photographed poorly. That disparity matters because tees are likely the first purchase for many customers, and the photography is setting a low price ceiling in their minds before they even click through to the technical gear.

**Advisor C (Strategy) — Framing the sale pricing as primarily a "strategic coherence" problem**

Advisor C is correct that the sale pricing creates a strategic coherence gap, but frames it primarily as a brand identity mismatch. From a visual design perspective, I'd argue the problem is more immediate and more damaging than strategic incoherence — it's an active trust signal failure happening on every page load, every product view, every collection browse. It's not just that "limited drops" and "perpetual sale" are philosophically inconsistent. It's that the visual presentation of a crossed-out price next to a sale badge is one of the most psychologically loaded design elements in ecommerce, and it's firing the wrong signal continuously. The fix isn't just strategic alignment — it's a visual design decision that needs to be made and implemented this week, not as part of a broader strategic review.

**Advisor H (Marketing) — Suggesting the Instagram bio needs restructuring**

Advisor H proposes a revised Instagram bio that removes the discount offer from the bio and restructures the copy. I understand the positioning rationale, but from a visual and conversion standpoint, the discount code in the bio ("10% OFF your 1st order | Code: FIRST") is actually doing functional work — it's a lead-conversion mechanism for people who've already decided they're interested. The problem with removing it is that the bio is currently one of the only places on the brand's digital presence that gives a first-time visitor a concrete reason to act. I'd argue the bio should be restructured to lead with identity (as Advisor H suggests) but retain the discount offer — just repositioned as the last line rather than the third line. The visual hierarchy of the bio matters: identity first, mission second, offer third, link fourth.

## Revisions to My Review

**What I Underweighted: The Photography Gap Between Product Categories**

My original review identified the t-shirt photography as weaker than the rashguard/shorts photography. What I didn't fully develop is the *strategic consequence* of this gap. Looking at the inventory data alongside the photography quality: the rashguard and fight shorts (well-photographed) have sold units. The Drop 1 tees (ghost mannequin, smoky background) have sold some units but have significant remaining inventory. The Stay Ready Oversized Tee (98 units, essentially no photography visible on the site) has sold zero units.

The correlation between photography quality and sell-through rate is not coincidental. Photography is the product experience online. The visual hierarchy of investment in photography should mirror the business priority of moving inventory. Right now it's inverted — the products with the most inventory sitting unsold (tees, oversized tee) have the weakest photography.

**Revised Photography Priority:**
1. **Immediate:** Get the Stay Ready Oversized Tee on a body in a lifestyle context. 98 units at $10.81 COGS sitting unsold is a photography problem as much as a marketing problem. This product has strong lifestyle appeal ("from the gym to the streets") that is completely invisible without lifestyle photography.
2. **Within 2 weeks:** Reshoot the Drop 1 tees on a model with the same red-background treatment used for the rashguard and fight shorts. The visual inconsistency between drops is costing tee conversions.
3. **Ongoing:** Every new drop should have consistent photography treatment before it goes live. The photography standard set by the rashguard page should be the floor, not the ceiling.

**What I Missed: The Logo Scaling Problem Needs a Specific Solution**

I flagged that the logo becomes illegible at small sizes but didn't provide a concrete fix. This is a significant omission. The solution is standard brand practice: develop a secondary logomark — just the snake illustration, without the wordmark — that functions as the small-scale brand identifier. The snake alone, at 40×40px, retains its character and reads clearly. The full wordmark + snake logo functions at large scale (hero sections, product graphics, packaging). The snake-only mark functions at small scale (favicon, header logo, social media profile photo, embroidery on apparel).

This is a one-time design task that costs nothing if Rosa has access to the original logo files, or a small freelance fee if she doesn't. It should be done before any paid advertising begins, because the logo will appear at small scale in ad thumbnails.
