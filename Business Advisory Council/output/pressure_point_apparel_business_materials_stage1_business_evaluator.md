## Unit Economics Assessment

### What We Know

You have sold approximately 39 units. You have given away 33 units (23 giveaways + 10 founder samples) at a combined COGS cost of $433.98. You have spent $8,213.30 in total across all categories. You have received $6,300 in gifts and loans.

**The critical problem: we cannot calculate a real CAC yet.**

Every sale to date has come through one of three channels: direct personal relationships, gifting/word-of-mouth seeding, or organic Instagram with 300 followers. There has been zero paid acquisition. This means your current "CAC" is essentially the cost of Rosa's time plus the giveaway investment — neither of which is being tracked in dollar terms.

**What we can model as a proxy:**

If we attribute the $433.98 in giveaway/sample costs as your primary demand-generation spend, and credit those 39 sales as the output:

- **Implied CAC (giveaway-driven):** $433.98 ÷ 39 = **~$11.13 per customer**

This number is misleading in both directions. It understates true CAC because it excludes Rosa's time. It also doesn't tell you whether those 39 customers came from the giveaways or from some other touchpoint. You don't know yet.

**What a healthy CAC looks like for this category:**

For a DTC apparel brand targeting a niche community, a sustainable CAC via paid social is typically $25–$60. For grassroots/community-driven brands with strong word-of-mouth, effective CAC can be held to $15–$30 if the community flywheel works. Your target should be a **3:1 LTV:CAC ratio minimum.**

### LTV Modeling

Your target customer buys "a few times a year around limited drops." Let's model conservatively and optimistically:

**Average Order Value (AOV) — blended across your product mix:**

Based on your margin table, if we assume a typical customer buys one tee or one technical piece:
- Tee-only buyer: ~$20–$25 AOV
- Technical gear buyer (rashguard or shorts): ~$45–$50 AOV
- Blended estimate: **~$35 AOV** (weighted toward tees given current sales mix)

**Repeat Purchase Rate:**

In grappling apparel, a brand with genuine community connection can achieve 2–3 purchases per year from loyal customers. A brand without that connection sees mostly one-and-done behavior. You are too early to know which you are. Let's model both:

| Scenario | Purchases/Year | Years Active | Gross LTV | Net Margin/Order | LTV (net) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | 1.5x/year | 2 years | 3 orders | ~$12 blended | **~$36** |
| Moderate | 2x/year | 2.5 years | 5 orders | ~$12 blended | **~$60** |
| Optimistic | 3x/year | 3 years | 9 orders | ~$12 blended | **~$108** |

**LTV:CAC Ratio Assessment:**

| LTV Scenario | At $15 CAC | At $30 CAC | At $50 CAC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative ($36) | 2.4:1 ❌ | 1.2:1 ❌ | 0.7:1 ❌ |
| Moderate ($60) | 4:1 ✅ | 2:1 ⚠️ | 1.2:1 ❌ |

**Finding:** The unit economics only work if you can either (a) keep CAC very low through community/organic channels, or (b) build genuine repeat purchase behavior that pushes LTV toward the moderate-to-optimistic range. At typical paid social CAC for apparel ($30–$50), the numbers are marginal to negative unless repeat purchase rates are strong.

This is why your gym partnership strategy is financially correct as a starting point — it's the only channel that can realistically hold CAC low enough for the economics to work right now.

**What you must start tracking immediately:**
- Which channel did each sale come from?
- Has any customer purchased more than once?
- What is the email open rate on your post-purchase sequence?
- Are any giveaway recipients converting to paid customers?

### The Shipping Problem Is Bigger Than It Looks

You are absorbing $3.50–$5.00 per order in shipping costs. On a $20 tee, that's 17.5–25% of your revenue going to subsidize shipping. This is a structural problem.

**On the Apply the Pressure Tee specifically:** At $20 retail with $9.88 COGS plus $5 shipping absorbed plus $0.88 in fees, your true net is $4.24 per unit. That is a 21% net margin. After allocating any portion of your $147/month in recurring costs, this product is barely covering its own weight.

**The "Sale" pricing is making this worse.** I can see from the website screenshots that multiple products are showing crossed-out prices ($30 → $20, $55 → $50, $50 → $45). You are already selling at discounted prices on products that have thin margins. Every percentage point of discount on a $20 tee with $16 in fully-loaded costs is significant. More on this in the red flags section.

### Where the Margin Is Actually Going

Ranked by margin erosion:

1. **Shipping subsidy** — $3.50–$5.00 per order. On low-ticket items this is the single biggest margin killer.
2. **COGS on technical products** — Rashguards at $25.75 COGS on a $50 retail price leave limited room for error.
3. **Shopify transaction fees** — 2.9% + $0.30 per transaction. On a $20 item that's $0.88 or 4.4% of revenue.
4. **Recurring overhead** — $147/month spread across 39 units sold in 4 months = ~$15/unit in overhead absorption. This number will compress dramatically as volume increases, but right now it's significant.
5. **Giveaway cost** — $433.98 in product given away. This is a marketing investment, not waste, but it needs to be tracked as CAC spend.

### The Overhead Absorption Reality

$147/month × 4 months = $588 in recurring costs since launch. Spread across 39 units sold = **$15.08 per unit in overhead.** Add this to your true net calculations above and most products are currently operating at a loss on a fully-loaded basis.

This is normal at 39 units. It is not sustainable past the point where you should be covering overhead. The overhead burden drops to manageable levels at approximately 50+ units per month.

## Revenue Model Evaluation

### Current Model: One-Time, Drop-Driven

Your revenue model is currently 100% dependent on new customer acquisition through drops. Every drop requires generating new demand. There is no recurring revenue, no subscription, and no confirmed repeat purchase behavior yet.

**Is this sustainable?**

The drop model can work — Shoyoroll built a business on it — but it requires either (a) a large enough community that each drop sells through without paid acquisition, or (b) paid acquisition economics that work. You currently have neither at scale.

**The drop model's financial characteristics:**
- Revenue is lumpy and unpredictable
- Inventory must be purchased before revenue is known
- Cash is tied up between drops
- Each drop is essentially a new launch event requiring marketing energy

**What needs to change:**

The path to sustainability runs through repeat purchase behavior. Your target customer buys "a few times a year." That means a customer who buys in Drop 1 should be buying again in Drop 2 or Drop 3. You need to know whether this is happening.

**Action required:** Tag every customer in Shopify by their first purchase date and drop number. When Drop 3 launches, track what percentage of Drop 1 and Drop 2 customers repurchase. If that number is above 20%, you have a real retention signal. If it's below 10%, you have a one-and-done problem that no amount of new drops will fix.

### The Charity Commitment's Revenue Model Impact

The 10% of gross profits to AFSP is non-negotiable and I respect that. Here is what it means financially:

On a rashguard sold at $50 with a true net profit of ~$17, 10% of gross profit (not net profit — this distinction matters) needs to be clarified. 

**If "gross profit" means revenue minus COGS only:**
- Gross profit on rashguard = $50 - $25.75 = $24.25

**If "gross profit" means net profit after all costs:**

Clarify this definition now and encode it in your dashboard. The difference compounds as volume grows. Either interpretation is workable at your current price points, but the math needs to be explicit.
