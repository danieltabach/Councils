## Agreements

**Advisor A (Financial) — The gym wholesale option is the most cash-efficient near-term channel.**

Advisor A correctly identifies that Option C (wholesale at $17/unit) generates immediate cash with zero shipping cost absorbed. My original analysis reached the same conclusion from a market positioning angle — the wholesale structure creates clean channel separation — but Advisor A's unit economics framing adds important precision. On a t-shirt at $17 wholesale versus $5.55 COGS, the net is actually competitive with DTC when you strip out the shipping subsidy Rosa is absorbing. The market data supports this: Tatami built its early distribution through exactly this kind of gym-first wholesale model before scaling online. This is a validated playbook, not an experiment.

**Advisor B (Customer) — "This brand needs to be felt before it's bought."**

This is the single most market-relevant insight in the entire council review, and I want to explicitly validate it from a market data perspective. In grappling apparel specifically, the purchase decision for technical gear (rashguards, shorts) is heavily influenced by tactile experience and peer validation. The customer's observation — that a gym partner wearing it and confirming quality would trigger purchase within a week — is consistent with how community-driven apparel brands in this category have historically converted customers. Tatami's early growth was almost entirely word-of-mouth from coaches and training partners. Scramble built its cult following through practitioners who encountered the product at competitions. The website is the wrong first touchpoint for this brand right now. The mat is correct. Market data validates this instinct completely.

**Advisor C (Future Visionary) — TikTok's discovery algorithm is structurally different from Instagram's and represents the highest-leverage organic acquisition channel available.**

Advisor C's framing of TikTok as a discovery engine versus Instagram as a distribution channel for existing audiences is market-accurate. The data on this is clear: TikTok's algorithm surfaces content to non-followers at a rate that Instagram Reels no longer matches for small accounts. For a brand with 300 followers and no paid budget, this is not a minor channel preference — it is the difference between reaching new customers and not reaching them. I should have been more emphatic about this in my original review. I mentioned TikTok Shop as a future opportunity but understated the urgency of the organic TikTok channel right now.

**Advisor E (Strategy) — The "limited drops" identity claim is not yet operationally supported by sell-through data.**

My original review flagged the perpetual sale pricing as a brand coherence problem, but Advisor E makes a sharper point: the scarcity narrative itself is not yet earned. 363 units of unsold inventory across multiple drops is not a scarcity model. This is a market perception risk I identified but understated. When a brand claims limited drops and customers can still easily find product from Drop 001 months later, the scarcity signal collapses. This matters for pricing power — scarcity is one of the primary mechanisms that justifies mid-premium pricing in this category (see Shoyoroll). Until sell-through rates improve dramatically, the brand should de-emphasize scarcity language and lean into community and identity messaging instead.

**Advisor G (Marketing) — The AFSP commitment is the most underutilized marketing asset in the business.**

I flagged the mental health advocacy angle as a tailwind in my original review, but Advisor G's specific observation — that it currently appears as a footnote when it should be a homepage section — is a market positioning failure I should have been more direct about. From a market differentiation standpoint, no other grappling apparel brand has a formalized, publicly committed mental health charity partnership. In a demographic (young men, 20-35) with documented elevated mental health risk, this is not a peripheral feature. It is a core differentiator that the market is not currently seeing because it's buried in the Our Story page.

**Advisor H (Conversion) — The awareness funnel is the primary problem, not on-site conversion.**

Advisor H's framing is market-correct: "You cannot convert someone who has never heard of you." At current traffic levels, optimizing the checkout flow is premature. The market-level problem is that the brand is invisible outside of a 300-follower Instagram account and a local gym network. This is consistent with my original assessment that the awareness problem precedes the conversion problem, but Advisor H's funnel mapping makes the sequencing clearer than I did.

## Disagreements

**Advisor A — The blended AOV estimate of ~$35 may be too conservative for strategic planning purposes.**

Advisor A uses a ~$35 blended AOV weighted toward tees. From a market benchmarking perspective, I would push back on this as a planning assumption. The brand's strategic intent is to be known for technical gear (rashguards, fight shorts) — these are the products with $45-50 price points and the strongest margin profiles. If the gym partnership strategy succeeds in getting practitioners to touch the product first, the conversion pathway is more likely to run through the rashguard than the tee. The target customer (male grappler, 1-3 years in) needs rashguards for training — they are a functional purchase, not just an identity purchase. A $50 rashguard is a more defensible AOV target than $35. Planning around $35 AOV may understate the unit economics potential if the channel strategy is executed correctly.

**Advisor C — The "community-as-moat" framing is correct directionally but the timeline is optimistic for a 2-person team.**

Advisor C's community-led brand model analysis is strategically sound, but the recommendation to build Discord infrastructure, membership models, and community containers is premature for a team of two with 363 units of unsold inventory. From a market sequencing perspective, community infrastructure requires a minimum viable audience to sustain it — typically 500-1,000 engaged members before a private community has enough critical mass to be self-sustaining. The brand is not there yet. The correct sequence is: gym partnerships → social proof accumulation → email list growth → community infrastructure. Building the container before the community exists is a resource allocation error that will drain Rosa's already-constrained time.

**Advisor D (Creative) — The sale pricing recommendation to "return to full price" needs market context.**

Advisor D recommends removing sale pricing and returning to original prices. I agree with the diagnosis (perpetual sale pricing undermines brand positioning) but the prescription needs market calibration. The original prices — $30 for tees, $55 for the rashguard — are at the upper end of what the market data supports for an unknown brand with zero reviews and no social proof. A $55 rashguard from a brand with no community validation is a harder sell than a $50 rashguard from the same brand. The correct resolution is not necessarily to restore the original prices — it is to commit to the current prices as the real prices, remove the sale badges, and stop the strikethrough pricing. The price point itself ($50 rashguard, $25 tee) is defensible. The sale framing is the problem, not the price level.

**Advisor G — The recommendation to deprioritize Facebook entirely may be premature for the gym partnership channel.**

Advisor G correctly identifies Facebook as a low-priority organic channel for reaching 20-35 year old male grapplers. However, from a market channel perspective, Facebook Groups remain one of the primary community platforms for local BJJ gym communities. Many gyms maintain active Facebook Groups for their members — event announcements, open mat schedules, competition results. For the gym partnership strategy specifically, having a Facebook presence that can be tagged in gym group posts is a low-cost distribution amplifier. This is not a content creation priority — it is a presence requirement for the gym partnership channel to have digital legs.

## Revisions to My Review

### What I Missed: The Geographic Concentration of the Opportunity

My original review correctly sized the national SAM at 250,000-400,000 potential customers, but I failed to make the geographic concentration point explicit enough. The four target gyms — Riverfront Jiu-Jitsu, ChesDel Jiu-Jitsu, Park Slope Academy, Roger Carpenter — are all in the New York/Philadelphia/Delaware corridor. This geographic concentration is actually a strategic asset at this stage, not a limitation.

The market data on community-driven apparel brands shows that geographic density of early adopters creates compounding word-of-mouth effects that dispersed customer bases do not. When multiple practitioners in the same regional training community are wearing the same brand, it creates visible social proof at the local level — practitioners see the brand on training partners, at regional competitions, at open mats. This is how Tatami built its early US presence: concentrated regional credibility before national expansion.
