## Agreements

**Advisor A (Customer Review) — Zero reviews is the #1 trust killer.**

Advisor A's visceral, first-person account of landing on the site and finding zero reviews confirms what I flagged in my audit. But A adds something I understated: the emotional specificity of what a single real review would accomplish. "Wore this for three rounds of no-gi, it didn't ride up, the logo held up after washing" — that's the exact format that converts. I identified the absence of reviews as a trust gap. A showed exactly how thin the line is between "I'm interested" and "I'll buy." The brand is one review away from closing a meaningful percentage of warm visitors. This point cannot be overstated.

**Advisor A — "This brand needs to be felt before it's bought."**

This is the most important customer insight in the entire council review and I did not surface it with sufficient clarity in my audit. A's conclusion — that the website is the wrong first touchpoint, the mat is the right first touchpoint — reframes the entire conversion problem. My audit treated the site as the primary conversion surface. A correctly identifies that for this brand, at this stage, the gym is where purchase intent is actually formed. The website is where it gets completed. That sequencing changes which fixes are most urgent.

**Advisor C (Financial) — The shipping subsidy is a structural problem, not just a checkout friction point.**

I flagged shipping cost visibility as a checkout abandonment cause. C goes further and correctly frames it as a margin erosion problem that compounds at scale. On the Apply the Pressure Tee specifically — $4.24 true net profit per unit — the shipping subsidy is consuming a disproportionate share of what little margin exists. C's framing of the wholesale Option C as a cash flow accelerator (immediate payment, zero shipping absorbed) is a point I touched on but didn't develop fully. The wholesale channel isn't just a distribution strategy — it's a cash flow fix.

**Advisor E (Strategy) — The "sale" pricing is strategically incoherent with the brand positioning.**

Multiple advisors flagged this, but E's framing is the most precise: it's not just a visual problem or a trust problem — it's a coherence problem. A brand that explicitly positions itself above the "$10-25 budget tier" is displaying t-shirts at $20 with a $30 strikethrough. The customer reads the $20 price, not the $30 original. The brand has priced itself into the tier it's trying to escape. I flagged this in my audit but E's strategic framing strengthens the argument considerably.

**Advisor F (Marketing) — The AFSP commitment is the most underutilized conversion asset on the site.**

I mentioned the mental health donation in my trust signals section but treated it as one signal among several. F correctly identifies it as the single most underutilized asset in the entire business. For a target customer (young male grapplers, 20-35) who statistically represents one of the highest-risk demographics for mental health struggles, this isn't a charity checkbox — it's a resonance trigger. F's specific recommendation — a dedicated homepage section, not a paragraph — is correct. I would add: it should also appear on every product page, near the Add to Cart button. "This purchase supports the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention" at the point of decision is a conversion lever, not just a brand statement.

**Advisor G (Creative) — The two competing visual languages on the site are actively undermining trust.**

G's diagnosis of "Language 1 vs. Language 2" — the dark/gritty brand expression versus the clean/generic Shopify template bleed-through — is a precise articulation of something I observed but didn't break down as clearly. The "Don't stop there!" section with its rounded sans-serif font appearing on every page is a specific, fixable execution gap that I should have called out explicitly. G is right that this inconsistency signals to the subconscious that the brand isn't fully formed.

**Advisor D (Future) — Single-channel Instagram dependency is the most acute strategic risk.**

D's point that Instagram's organic reach for small brand accounts is in structural decline — and that a 300-follower account is essentially invisible to new audiences — validates my assessment that the awareness funnel is critically closed. D adds the forward-looking dimension: this problem gets worse over time, not better, without deliberate channel diversification. TikTok's discovery algorithm is the correct near-term fix, and multiple advisors converge on this.

## Disagreements

**Advisor F — The Instagram bio rewrite.**

F recommends changing the bio to lead with "Pressure Is a Privilege." followed by "Minimalist gear for grapplers who persevere." and "10% of profits → mental health advocacy."

I partially disagree on the "10% of profits" line. The AFSP commitment is 10% of **gross profits**, not revenue. "10% of profits" is technically accurate but could be read as a larger commitment than it is (if a customer assumes "profits" means revenue). More importantly, from a conversion mechanics standpoint, the bio is not where this commitment converts — the product page is. The bio should drive clicks to the site. The AFSP message should close the sale on the product page. Putting it in the bio uses it as an awareness signal when its highest value is as a conversion signal. I'd keep the bio tighter and surface the AFSP commitment more aggressively on product pages instead.

**Advisor C — The LTV modeling assumes blended AOV of ~$35.**

C's blended AOV estimate of ~$35 is reasonable given the current sales mix, but it may be systematically underestimating the brand's actual opportunity. The target customer who converts through a gym partnership — who has physically touched the product, seen it on a coach, and bought with high intent — is more likely to be a rashguard/shorts buyer ($45-50) than a tee buyer ($20-25). The gym channel self-selects for higher-ticket purchases because practitioners who are buying gear for training need technical gear, not lifestyle tees. If gym partnerships become the primary acquisition channel (as they should be), the blended AOV should trend upward over time. C's conservative model is correct for current DTC traffic but may understate the economics of the gym-first strategy.

**Advisor H (Market) — The SAM estimate of 250,000-400,000 US customers.**

H's serviceable addressable market estimate is directionally correct but the 15-20% psychographic filter may be too conservative. H applies this filter to identify grapplers who specifically value "identity-driven, minimalist branding" — but the relevant filter for conversion purposes is simpler: grapplers who are willing to buy apparel from a brand they discover through their gym or social community. That's a broader filter. The brand doesn't need the customer to be a minimalism enthusiast — it needs them to be a grappler who encounters the brand in a credible context (a coach wearing it, a training partner recommending it) and finds the product appealing. The addressable market for that customer is larger than H's estimate suggests. This matters for the financial modeling: the ceiling is higher than the conservative SAM implies.

**Advisor G — Packaging score of 4/10.**

G gives packaging a 4/10 and frames it primarily as a brand experience gap. From a pure conversion mechanics standpoint, I'd score it differently. Packaging affects post-purchase satisfaction and repeat purchase behavior — it's a retention lever, not a first-purchase conversion lever. At 39 units sold with a 2-person team and no paid acquisition, the packaging investment priority is lower than G implies. The thank you card recommendation is correct and high-ROI. But the broader packaging upgrade G implies would require budget and operational capacity that should be directed elsewhere right now. I'd score packaging at 5/10 for this stage — functional, with one high-leverage fix (the thank you card) that costs nothing additional to improve.

## Revisions to My Review

**What I missed: The gym partnership as a conversion surface, not just an awareness channel.**

My original audit treated the gym partnerships primarily as a traffic/awareness play — a way to get the brand in front of qualified customers who would then convert on the website. The website conversion mechanics I audited are secondary to this physical touchpoint.
