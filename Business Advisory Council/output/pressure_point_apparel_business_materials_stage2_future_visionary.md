## AGREEMENTS

### Advisor B (Marketing) — TikTok as Primary Organic Acquisition Channel

Advisor B's framing of Instagram as a distribution channel for people who already know you versus TikTok as a discovery channel for people who don't is precisely correct — and it has strategic implications that extend well beyond the next 30 days. The algorithmic architecture of TikTok is fundamentally different from Instagram's, and that difference matters enormously for a brand at this stage. But I want to extend B's point into the future arc: **TikTok is not just the right channel for now — it is the channel that will matter most for the 2027-2029 window when social commerce fully matures.**

TikTok Shop's integration of content and commerce is the leading edge of a structural shift in how apparel brands sell. The brands that build algorithmic history, content libraries, and community on TikTok now will have compounding advantages when TikTok Shop becomes the dominant purchase channel for the 18-35 demographic. Every month of delay is not just a missed marketing opportunity — it is a missed infrastructure investment.

I agree with B's urgency. I would amplify it: this is not a channel choice, it is a platform bet on where commerce is going.

### Advisor C (Strategy) — The "Sale" Pricing Problem as a Brand Coherence Issue

Advisor C correctly identifies the perpetual sale pricing as a strategic incoherence, not merely a tactical misstep. I agree completely, and I want to name the future-state risk that C gestures toward but doesn't fully articulate: **brands that train their early customers to expect discounts create a pricing ceiling that is extraordinarily difficult to raise later.**

The customers Pressure Point is acquiring right now — at $20 tees and $50 rashguards with strikethrough pricing — are forming price anchors. When the brand attempts to move to full-price positioning at scale, those early customers will experience the price increase as a betrayal rather than a maturation. This is not hypothetical. It is the documented failure mode of brands like Nasty Gal, early Everlane, and dozens of DTC apparel brands that built their initial audience on promotional pricing and then couldn't escape it.

The time to fix this is now, at 39 sales, not at 3,900.

### Advisor D (Conversion) — The Welcome Sequence as Highest-ROI Fix

Advisor D's identification of the missing welcome sequence as a critical conversion leak is correct, and I want to add a future-state dimension: **the welcome sequence is not just an email automation — it is the first chapter of the community narrative that will define the brand's long-term retention architecture.**

The brands that win in the community-led model don't just sell products — they onboard members. The welcome sequence is where that onboarding begins. Written correctly, it doesn't just convert a subscriber into a first-time buyer; it converts a curious visitor into someone who understands what they're joining. That distinction matters enormously for LTV, which is the metric that will determine whether this brand can sustain paid acquisition when it eventually needs to.

### Advisor G (Customer) — "This Brand Needs to Be Felt Before It's Bought"

This is the most strategically important sentence in the entire advisory council output, and it comes from the customer perspective rather than any strategic framework. Advisor G's observation that the gym partnership strategy makes sense because physical contact with the product reduces the trust deficit that the website cannot yet overcome — this is a profound insight that has long-term implications.

It points toward a future where Pressure Point's distribution architecture is fundamentally experiential before it is digital. The brands that build lasting community in niche athletic markets — Represent, Satisfy Running, Ten Thousand — all have a physical touchpoint layer: events, gym partnerships, athlete relationships, pop-ups. The website is not the primary conversion engine for these brands. The community is. The website is where you go after you've already decided.

Pressure Point is, perhaps accidentally, building the right architecture. The gym partnerships are not just a near-term sales channel — they are the experiential layer that will make the digital channel work.

## DISAGREEMENTS

### Advisor E (Financial) — The LTV Modeling Understates the Community-Led Brand Potential

Advisor E's LTV modeling is technically sound but strategically conservative in a way that may lead to incorrect strategic conclusions. The model assumes a blended net margin of ~$12 per order and 1.5-3 purchases per year, yielding a conservative-to-optimistic LTV range of $36-$108.

**My disagreement:** This model is built on the assumption that Pressure Point behaves like a standard DTC apparel brand with normal repeat purchase dynamics. But the brands that Pressure Point is most analogous to — community-led, philosophy-driven, drop-model brands — exhibit dramatically different LTV curves. Shoyoroll customers don't buy 2-3 times per year; they buy every drop and spend $180+ per purchase. Represent's community members don't churn; they become brand evangelists who recruit new customers at zero CAC.

The strategic question is not "what is the LTV of a standard apparel customer?" It is "what is the LTV of a community member who identifies with the Pressure Point philosophy?" Those are different numbers. The second number is potentially 3-5x the first, but only if the brand successfully builds the community infrastructure that converts customers into members.

Advisor E's model is correct for the brand as it currently exists. It may significantly understate the potential of the brand as it should exist in 3-5 years. Strategic decisions made on the basis of conservative LTV assumptions may underinvest in community infrastructure that would dramatically improve those numbers.

### Advisor B (Marketing) — The Influencer Targeting Criteria Are Too Narrow

Advisor B recommends targeting BJJ practitioners with 2,000-20,000 followers whose tone is "thoughtful or philosophical, not aggressive." I understand the logic — brand alignment matters — but this targeting criterion may be too restrictive for the current stage.

**My disagreement:** At 39 sales and 300 followers, Pressure Point needs reach more than it needs perfect brand alignment. A BJJ practitioner with 8,000 followers who posts primarily technique content — not philosophy content — still has the exact target customer in their audience. The brand's philosophy doesn't need to be pre-reflected in the influencer's content; it needs to be introduced to the influencer's audience.

More importantly, from a future-state perspective: **the most valuable influencer relationships for a brand like Pressure Point are not with people who already sound like the brand — they are with people who have credibility in the community and whose authentic endorsement carries weight precisely because they don't endorse everything.** A technique-focused BJJ creator with 15,000 followers who genuinely likes the rashguard and says so is more valuable than a philosophy-aligned creator with 5,000 followers who posts similar content to Pressure Point's own feed.

The targeting should be: grappling community credibility + authentic product fit + audience that matches the target customer. Philosophy alignment in the creator's content is a nice-to-have, not a requirement.
