## Disagreements

**Advisor B on EMNLP 2026 being your "best venue."** B makes a confident venue recommendation toward EMNLP Budapest but the reasoning is primarily about thematic fit. I'd push back on this as the primary target for your *current* situation. EMNLP workshops are competitive and the NLP community will scrutinize your statistical methodology more harshly than an interdisciplinary workshop would. Your Spearman ρ computed over non-independent observations, your acknowledged inflation of ε² in the deterministic condition, your researcher-constructed word scale — these are all things an NLP reviewer will go after. A NeurIPS workshop with a more applied/deployment focus is actually a more forgiving venue for a behavioral measurement paper at this stage. Get the workshop acceptance first, then target EMNLP for the extended version.

**Advisor C on the "three-paper arc" framing:** C's sequencing is sensible but I think it subtly misleads you about what you actually need to do. The framing of "Paper 2, Paper 3, Paper 4" implies a clean linear progression. What I've seen kill research programs like yours — part-time, solo, no institutional support — is the gap between papers. You don't need a three-paper arc. You need one tight, defensible paper that gets accepted somewhere real. Then you reassess. The arc framing encourages scope creep. The valence/stakes study is genuinely interesting but it's a different paper with a different experimental design. Don't let it bleed into what you're submitting in August.

**Advisor A on the adversarial reading of state dominance:** A argues that the ε² ratio of 10:1 is "a consequence of your experimental design, not a discovery about language models" because you chose to include an 89% ceiling condition. This is partially right but overstated. The *inversion* — word dominates at low baselines, context dominates at high baselines — is not mechanically forced by the design. You could have found that word choice matters equally at all baselines, or that context always dominates. The regime change finding is real. A is correct that the overall ratio is sensitive to which baseline values you include, and that should be acknowledged more explicitly. But dismissing the finding as purely design-driven is too strong.

**Multiple advisors on mechanistic interpretability:** Several advisors (A, C, E) recommend against mech interp given your background. I agree with the conclusion but want to add a more specific reason they didn't fully articulate: you cannot do mech interp on Claude Haiku because you don't have the weights. The entire mech interp toolkit — activation patching, attention head analysis, TransformerLens — requires open weights. If you go this direction, you're running a completely different experiment on a completely different model, which means your existing results don't connect to the mech interp findings. That's not a minor obstacle. It's a design discontinuity that would require you to re-establish your behavioral findings on an open model first, then do the mech interp on the same model. That's 6+ months of work minimum.

## Blind Spots

**The replication crisis problem that nobody mentioned.** Your entire experiment runs on one model at one point in time via API. Claude Haiku is a commercial model that gets updated without announcement. If someone tries to replicate your results in six months and gets different numbers because Anthropic updated the model weights, your paper is unfalsifiable. This is a genuine problem for API-based behavioral research and the field is increasingly aware of it. You should log the exact model version (you have `claude-haiku-4-5-20251001` — good), but you should also explicitly address in the paper that API model behavior can drift and that your results are time-stamped to a specific model version. This is a methodological disclosure that reviewers at serious venues are starting to require.

**The task framing confound that nobody fully addressed.** Your allocation task involves "innovation hours for architects." This is a specific professional domain with specific connotations. The model has training data about architects, innovation, and professional resource allocation. The compression pattern you observe might be specific to this domain framing — not because of the words, but because the model has learned that "innovation" is a vague, aspirational concept that gets hedged at 50% in professional contexts. The cross-model comparison helps here, but it doesn't resolve this. A within-paper robustness check using a different domain (even something abstract like "resource X in system Y") would substantially strengthen the claim that you're measuring word interpretation rather than domain-specific behavior.
